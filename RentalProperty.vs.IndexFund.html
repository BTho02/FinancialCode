What this model assumes

  • Both strategies start with the same cash: the property's down payment plus closing costs is treated as the index fund's lump-sum investment.
  • Whenever the property runs a cash-flow shortfall in a given month, that same amount is treated as also going into the index fund that month — the two strategies always draw on the same amount of outside cash. By default, months where the property is cash-flow positive, that surplus just accumulates as uninvested savings on the property side; check "Invest surplus cash flow in the S&P 500" to have it invested and compounding instead, using the same return assumptions as the index fund side.
  • In backtest mode, the mortgage rate is locked in at whatever the historical rate was in the start year, matching how a real fixed-rate loan works — appreciation and market return still move year to year with the historical record.
  • Historical figures are annual national averages (S&P CoreLogic Case-Shiller U.S. National Home Price Index, Freddie Mac PMMS 30-year fixed rate, S&P 500 total return as a proxy for VOO before its 2010 launch), except rent in the trailing-history comparison, which follows Wisconsin's actual median-rent trend — any specific property, fund, or local rental market will deviate from these, sometimes by a lot.
  • The tax estimate is a simplified capital-gains calculation at a single exit — it ignores mortgage-interest deductions, depreciation recapture, 1031 exchanges, and fund-level tax-loss harvesting, all of which can change a real outcome noticeably.
  • This is a comparison of assumptions you control, not a forecast or financial advice — small changes to appreciation, return, or tax inputs can flip which side wins.
